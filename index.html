Project 1

Does Weather Explain Air Quality?

Clustering US counties by air quality and climate

The question

Los Angeles is infamous for its smog. Miami is hotter and more humid, so I wanted to test whether weather drives air quality.

The data

Air quality: EPA annual AQI summaries for 2025. We measured Median AQI, 90th Percentile AQI, and the share of days rated "Good" (good ratio).

Weather: Mean temperature, humidity and total precipitation: NOAA Local Climatological Data for 2025, using one airport station per state.

Counties: Six large counties from six states: Los Angeles (CA), Harris (TX), Queens (NY), Cook (IL), Miami-Dade (FL) and Denver (CO).

What I did

  • Cleaned and combined the two sources, filling missing weather values with the average of that measure.
  • Checked correlations first, to see whether weather and air quality moved together before clustering anything.
  • Explored each variable with histograms, boxplots and a temperature-vs-AQI scatter plot.
  • Scaled every feature to 0–1. Total rainfall ranges in the hundreds to over a thousand, while the other variables are far smaller, so without scaling rainfall would have dominated the clustering.
  • Ran K-Means with k = 3.

What we found

Air quality measures move together; weather barely moves with them. Median AQI and 90th Percentile AQI correlate at 0.96, and the good-day ratio moves in the opposite direction (−0.91 and −0.83). But mean temperature and Median AQI correlate at only 0.17.

Project 2 · COMP3115

Two Dimensions of Dirty Air

Reducing 14 air-quality indicators with PCA · Group project, COMP3115 · Mar–Apr 2026 · My role: PCA and clustering in PCA space

The question

County air-quality data contains many similar measures, so I wanted to identify the key differences between counties and visualize them clearly.

The data

EPA annual air-quality summaries for counties across the six states, with 14 indicators per county, including AQI category days, AQI maximum, 90th percentile and median, and days dominated by CO, NO₂, ozone, PM2.5 and PM10.

What I did

  • Standardised all 14 indicators (z-scores), so days and AQI points could be compared fairly.
  • Ran a full PCA to see how much information each component keeps.
  • Projected counties onto the first two components and plotted them.
  • Used the elbow method to choose four clusters, then ran K-Means in this reduced space.
  • Built a biplot to see which original indicators push each component.

What I found

The two dimensions have clear meanings:

  • PC1 – general air quality: driven by the number of PM2.5 days, median AQI and unhealthy-air indicators. Counties on one side have generally cleaner air, counties on the other side have generally worse air.
  • PC2 – extreme events: driven by maximum AQI and upper-percentile values. A county can have a decent typical year and still suffer a few severe pollution days.

The key insight: Counties differ in two separate ways. Some are bad every day, others are mostly fine but hit by severe episodes. Looking only at average AQI would hide the second group.

Tools

Python, pandas, scikit-learn (StandardScaler, PCA, KMeans), matplotlib, seaborn

Project 3 · COMP2016

A Flight Booking Database

Oracle SQL and Python · Group project, COMP2016 · [placeholder: months/year] · My role: [placeholder: confirm role, e.g. database design, security and documentation]

The question

How to simulate a database system of flight booking?

The data

Four related tables: Customers, Flights, Bookings and Booking_Details. Booking_Details lets one booking hold up to three flights, in order. The system was set up with three sample customers and three sample flights: Hong Kong to Tokyo (CX100), Tokyo to New York (CX101) and Hong Kong to Beijing (CX102).

What I did

  1. Designed the schema and ER diagram: primary and foreign keys, a UNIQUE passport number so one passport cannot create two accounts, and a composite key on Booking_Details so the same flight cannot be added to a booking twice.
  2. Put business rules inside the database, not just the app: CHECK constraints reject negative fares, negative seat counts, and arrival times earlier than departure.
  3. Added a trigger that subtracts one seat every time a flight is added to a booking, so seat counts stay correct even if a booking is inserted outside the Python app.
  4. Built a Python command-line app to manage flights, search, book and cancel. Search finds direct flights and one-stop connections where the second flight leaves after the first arrives and the total travel time stays within the customer's limit; connections get a 10% discount.
  5. Used parameterised queries so user input is never run as SQL, and wrapped each booking in a transaction that rolls back if any flight fails.
  6. Cancelling a booking deletes it, and through ON DELETE CASCADE its flight details too.

How to read it

  1. Customers make Bookings (one customer → many bookings).
  2. A Booking has one or more Booking_Details rows (up to three flights).
  3. Booking_Details links each flight to the booking, in order.
  4. PK = primary key, FK = foreign key, U = unique constraint.

Entity-relationship diagram: customers make bookings, bookings contain booking details, and booking details link to flights.

What I found

A worked example with the sample data: there is no direct flight from Hong Kong to New York, so the search combines CX100 (12:00–16:00) with CX101 (18:30–23:30). The fare is (2000 + 4000) × 0.9 = 5400 and the trip takes 11.5 hours. Booking both flights takes one seat from each. CX102 has only one seat, so a second booking on it would be rejected by the seat check and the whole booking rolled back.

Key insight: putting the rules in the database, not only in the Python app, keeps the data correct no matter which program writes to it.
